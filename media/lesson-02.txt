An interview can supply both facts and the source’s interpretation. Facts explain what happened. A useful quotation adds a person’s perspective, feeling or explanation in their own words. Follow-up questions uncover detail that a broad opening question misses.

How to do it.
Step 1. Ask about a specific moment rather than requesting a generic reaction.
Step 2. Follow an answer with a question about the action, reason or consequence.
Step 3. Check factual details separately and preserve the source’s meaning when selecting a quote.

Worked example.
Start with the moment. Instead of “Was it fun?” ask “What did you change after the bridge fell?” The first question invites a yes or no. The second invites the source to describe a particular decision.
Follow the useful answer. ANSWER: “I moved the support.” FOLLOW-UP: “What made you try that position?” The follow-up seeks the reasoning that viewers cannot see in a photograph or infer from a result.
Choose what adds perspective. “I stopped trying to make it look straight and watched where it bent.” This practice quote explains a shift in thinking. Preserve the full interview context and verify its factual references.

Watch for this. An interesting quotation is not permission to remove a qualification that changes what the person meant.
