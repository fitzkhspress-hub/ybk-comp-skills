A primary headline attracts attention and names a meaningful idea. A secondary headline can explain the specific angle. When a layout uses both, they should add to each other rather than repeat a clever phrase. Accuracy is the foundation of either role.

How to do it.
Step 1. Identify the actual angle before writing a headline.
Step 2. Try a concise, specific primary line that fits the meaning.
Step 3. Use the supporting line to add verified information the primary line leaves out.

Worked example.
Start with the angle. ANGLE: Maya revised a bridge support after a failed test. The headline needs to reflect this reported focus, not claim a victory or record the reporting does not establish.
Give the primary line a job. PRIMARY: “A shift in support” The phrase links to an actual adjustment and suggests a change in approach. Cleverness remains accountable to the story.
Add information below. SECONDARY: “A failed first test led Maya to change her bridge design.” The supporting line provides the concrete focus. It adds information instead of restating “a shift” with different words.

Watch for this. A familiar slogan can sound polished while telling readers nothing specific about this coverage.
