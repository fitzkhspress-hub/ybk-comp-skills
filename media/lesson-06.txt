A caption connects the people and action in a photograph with information the picture cannot establish on its own. Visual identification, researched context and a meaningful source perspective can work together. Use your staff’s caption structure rather than treating this lesson as a sentence-count rule.

How to do it.
Step 1. Identify the relevant people and action from verified reporting.
Step 2. Add context the picture alone cannot prove, such as the stage of the process or why the action mattered.
Step 3. Check any quotation and match the finished caption unmistakably to its photograph.

Worked example.
Separate seeing from knowing. VISIBLE: Maya adjusts a support. REPORTED: this happened after the first test failed. A photo can show an action; it cannot establish the full sequence or identify a person without reliable information.
Add the missing context. “Maya adjusted the support after the first bridge collapsed during the class test.” The context adds meaning beyond “a student works on a project.” The name and sequence must be checked.
Use a voice when it contributes. “I watched where it bent instead of guessing,” Maya explained in the practice interview. The quotation explains a decision. Apply the staff’s attribution and caption format to actual work rather than copying the example as a template.

Watch for this. A caption should not assume an emotion just because a face appears to express it.
