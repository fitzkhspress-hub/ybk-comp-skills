A lead gives readers a reason to enter the story. A concrete, verified action can reveal the people and tension without announcing that the event was exciting. Description must come from observation or reliable reporting, not an imagined scene.

How to do it.
Step 1. Locate a revealing action, detail or exchange in your source notes.
Step 2. Choose a clear subject and a specific verb.
Step 3. Check every descriptive detail against the reporting and connect the opening to the story’s focus.

Worked example.
Read the notes first. NOTES: Maya moved a cardboard support; she watched the middle bend; the next test held. The notes establish a visible action. They do not establish sweat, silence, fear or any other unrecorded detail.
Choose an entry point. DRAFT: “The engineering challenge was an exciting learning experience.” The draft summarizes an evaluation rather than showing an experience. It could describe almost any event.
Write the supported action. “Maya slid the cardboard support toward the bend and tested the bridge again.” The opening gives readers a person and action. It creates interest through a reported decision rather than invented atmosphere.

Watch for this. A vivid lead is still inaccurate if its sensory details were never observed or confirmed.
