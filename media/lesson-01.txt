A topic names an event. An angle identifies something specific about how people experienced it. Reporting becomes more useful when it follows a person’s action, decision or consequence rather than assuming everyone had the same experience.

How to do it.
Step 1. Begin with an approved topic and ask who made a decision or experienced a change.
Step 2. Observe a specific action and ask the people involved to explain it.
Step 3. Write a focus supported by those details; revise it when the reporting points elsewhere.

Worked example.
Separate topic and angle. TOPIC: bridge challenge. ANGLE: Maya changed a support after her first test. The topic tells us what happened. The angle gives us a person, a decision and a process we can investigate.
Gather the evidence. SOURCE NOTES: first test failed; Maya moved a support; the next bridge held 18 washers. These notes support a concrete sequence. They do not establish that everyone loved the challenge or felt proud.
State a reportable focus. Follow how Maya diagnosed the failed test and changed her design. The focus comes from reported action. It gives interviews, photographs and copy a shared purpose without prescribing a story type.

Watch for this. Do not substitute an event slogan or a claim about everyone’s feelings for reporting.
